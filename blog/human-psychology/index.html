🧠 1. Decision Making & Cognitive Biases
#01

Confirmation Bias

Only Believing What Already Fits Our Beliefs

Decision Making

Our brains love being right. We naturally search for, notice, and remember facts that confirm what we already believe, while completely ignoring evidence that proves us wrong.

🎯 Everyday Example: If you believe a certain diet is healthy, you will read every article praising it and skip articles pointing out its health risks.
💡 Key Lesson: Actively ask yourself: 'What evidence would prove my belief wrong?'

#02

Anchoring Effect

Getting Trapped by the First Number You Hear

Decision Making

When making decisions, our mind relies heavily on the very first piece of information offered (the 'anchor'), even if that number is completely random or irrelevant.

🎯 Everyday Example: A store tags a jacket as '$200' with a line crossed through it, selling it for '$80'. You feel like you got a bargain, even if the jacket is only worth $50.
💡 Key Lesson: Always research true market value before entering any price negotiation.
More Info ↗
#03

Sunk Cost Fallacy

Holding on Just Because You Already Invested

Decision Making

We hate wasting money, time, or effort that we already spent. So we continue doing something bad or painful just because we don't want our past investment to feel wasted.

🎯 Everyday Example: Sitting through a boring 3-hour movie until the bitter end simply because you already paid $15 for the cinema ticket.
💡 Key Lesson: Money and time already spent are gone forever. Only judge future value.
More Info ↗
#04

Loss Aversion (Prospect Theory)

Losing $100 Hurts Twice as Much as Winning $100 Feels Good

Decision Making

Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky showed that the emotional pain of losing something is twice as strong as the joy of gaining the exact same thing.

🎯 Everyday Example: You feel far more angry and frustrated losing $50 from your wallet than you feel joyful finding a $50 note on the sidewalk.
💡 Key Lesson: Fear of loss often prevents people from taking smart, calculated risks in career and life.
More Info ↗
#05

Availability Heuristic

Confusing 'Memorable' with 'Common'

Decision Making

If something can easily be recalled from memory (because it was dramatic, scary, or on the news), we mistakenly assume it happens all the time.

🎯 Everyday Example: After watching news reports about a plane crash, people get terrified of flying and drive long distances instead, even though driving is statistically 100 times more dangerous.
💡 Key Lesson: Don't judge safety or likelihood by dramatic headlines; check actual statistics.
More Info ↗
#06

The Framing Effect

How You Word a Choice Dictates the Answer

Decision Making

People react differently to the exact same information depending on whether it is presented as a gain (positive) or a loss (negative).

🎯 Everyday Example: Patients prefer surgery with a '90% survival rate' over surgery with a '10% mortality rate', even though both numbers mean the exact same thing.
💡 Key Lesson: Reframe problems in both positive and negative lights before making up your mind.
More Info ↗
#07

Dunning-Kruger Effect

Beginners Feel Like Experts; Experts Feel Inadequate

Decision Making

People with very little knowledge in a field vastly overestimate their competence, while genuine experts tend to underestimate their abilities and assume everyone knows what they know.

🎯 Everyday Example: Someone watches a 10-minute YouTube video on cryptocurrency and starts advising their family like a seasoned Wall Street trader.
💡 Key Lesson: True mastery starts with the humility to admit how much you still don't know.
More Info ↗
#08

Hick's Law

More Options = Slower Decisions

Decision Making

The time it takes to make a decision increases logarithmically with the number and complexity of choices available. Too many choices leads to decision paralysis.

🎯 Everyday Example: A restaurant menu with 120 items leaves customers stressed and undecided, while a minimalist menu with 5 chef specials gets orders in seconds.
💡 Key Lesson: Simplify choices for yourself and your customers. Remove friction to take action.
More Info ↗
#09

The Decoy Effect (Asymmetric Dominance)

Adding a Bad Third Option to Force Your Choice

Decision Making

When choosing between two items, introducing a third, poorly priced 'decoy' makes one of the original items look like an irresistible bargain.

🎯 Everyday Example: Small Popcorn: $3, Medium Popcorn: $6.50, Large Popcorn: $7. Almost everyone buys the Large, because the Medium was deliberately priced high to act as bait.
💡 Key Lesson: Spot the decoy when purchasing subscriptions or meals to avoid paying for excess.
More Info ↗
#10

The Gambler's Fallacy

Believing Past Random Events Change Future Odds

Decision Making

The false belief that if an independent random event happens frequently in a row, the opposite outcome is 'due' to happen soon.

🎯 Everyday Example: A coin lands on 'Heads' 5 times in a row, so a gambler bets their savings on 'Tails', ignoring that the odds are still exactly 50/50.
💡 Key Lesson: Random processes have no memory. Every roll or coin flip is completely independent.

👥 2. Social Psychology & Group Dynamics
#11

The Bystander Effect

The More People Watching, the Less Anyone Helps

Social & Groups

Individuals are much less likely to offer help to a victim when other people are present. Everyone assumes someone else will call for help or step in.

🎯 Everyday Example: A person collapses on a busy city street with hundreds of pedestrians, and people just walk past assuming another person already called the ambulance.
💡 Key Lesson: In an emergency, point directly at one specific person: 'You in the blue shirt, call 911!'
More Info ↗
#12

The Halo Effect

One Attractive Trait Makes Us Think Everything is Great

Social & Groups

When we notice one positive quality in someone (such as physical attractiveness or charisma), our brain automatically assumes they are also kind, honest, and intelligent.

🎯 Everyday Example: A well-dressed, handsome job applicant is immediately perceived as trustworthy and hardworking during an interview, even before answering a single technical question.
💡 Key Lesson: Separate appearance and charming presentation from competence and character.
More Info ↗
#13

Social Proof

When Unsure, We Copy What Others Are Doing

Social & Groups

In unfamiliar or ambiguous situations, people look to others for cues on how to behave, assuming that the crowd must know the right thing to do.

🎯 Everyday Example: In a new city, tourists always choose the restaurant with a line out the door over the empty restaurant next door.
💡 Key Lesson: Popular doesn't always mean correct. Think independently before following the herd.
More Info ↗
#14

The Pygmalion Effect (Rosenthal Effect)

High Expectations Lead to Higher Performance

Social & Groups

When a leader, teacher, or coach genuinely expects great things from someone, that person unconsciously adopts those standards and improves their real-world results.

🎯 Everyday Example: Teachers were told randomly chosen students were 'blooming geniuses'. One year later, those students showed dramatic, measurable IQ gains because teachers gave them more encouragement.
💡 Key Lesson: Believe in your team and students; your genuine belief elevates their performance.
More Info ↗
#15

Rule of Reciprocity

The Irresistible Urge to Return a Favor

Social & Groups

Human society is built on mutual aid. When someone does something nice or gives us an unsolicited gift, we feel a deep psychological pressure to repay them.

🎯 Everyday Example: Charities that include free address labels or a small keychain in their donation letters receive double the donations of letters sent without a gift.
💡 Key Lesson: Give value first without expecting an immediate return; reciprocity will follow.
More Info ↗
#16

Fundamental Attribution Error

They Are Mean; I Was Just Stressed

Social & Groups

We blame other people's mistakes on their bad character ('He cut me off because he's a jerk!'), but we blame our own mistakes on external bad luck ('I cut him off because I was rushing to hospital!').

🎯 Everyday Example: A coworker is late to a meeting and you think they are lazy and irresponsible. When you are late, you blame traffic.
💡 Key Lesson: Give others the same benefit of the doubt you always grant yourself.
More Info ↗
#17

The Spotlight Effect

Nobody is Staring at You as Much as You Think

Social & Groups

We tend to believe that everyone is noticing our clothes, small flaws, mistakes, and awkward moments, when in reality most people are too busy worrying about themselves.

🎯 Everyday Example: You spill a drop of coffee on your shirt and spend the whole party feeling mortified, yet not a single person at the party even noticed it.
💡 Key Lesson: Liberate yourself: everyone is the main character in their own story, not yours.
More Info ↗
#18

Door-in-the-Face Technique

Ask for Something Impossible, Then Ask for What You Want

Social & Groups

The persuader starts by making a huge request that the person will surely refuse ('slam the door'). Then they follow up with their real, smaller request, which now sounds completely reasonable.

🎯 Everyday Example: 'Can you volunteer 10 hours every week for a year?' 'No way!' 'Okay, could you just donate $10 today?' 'Sure, I can do that.'
💡 Key Lesson: Recognize when a seller or negotiator is anchoring high just to make their target deal look cheap.
More Info ↗
#19

Foot-in-the-Door Technique

Start with a Tiny Favor to Win a Big One Later

Social & Groups

Getting a person to agree to a small, effortless request first dramatically increases the likelihood that they will later agree to a much larger, related request.

🎯 Everyday Example: First asking neighbors to sign a petition for safe driving (small). Two weeks later, asking to put a huge billboard on their lawn (big) — agreement tripled!
💡 Key Lesson: Build commitments gradually; micro-actions create momentum.
More Info ↗
#20

In-Group Bias (Us vs. Them)

Giving Unfair Favor to Your Own Tribe

Social & Groups

Humans form group identities over anything (sports, politics, programming languages) and naturally favor people in their own group while distrusting outsiders.

🎯 Everyday Example: In an office, employees from the engineering department immediately defend each other and critique the marketing team without reviewing the actual facts.
💡 Key Lesson: Focus on shared common goals across departments and groups to dismantle tribal friction.

⚡ 3. Habits, Work & Motivation Laws
#21

Parkinson's Law

Work Expands to Fill Whatever Time You Give It

Habits & Motivation

If you give yourself 2 weeks to finish a 2-hour report, the task will mentally expand in complexity and stress until it eats up the entire 2 weeks.

🎯 Everyday Example: A student given 30 days for an essay waits until day 29 to write it in an all-nighter, producing the exact same quality as if given 48 hours.
💡 Key Lesson: Set short, tight deadlines. Force simplicity and speed by constraining time.
More Info ↗
#22

Yerkes-Dodson Law

The Sweet Spot Between Boredom and Panic

Habits & Motivation

Performance increases with physiological or mental arousal (pressure), but only up to an optimal peak. Too little stress causes laziness; too much stress causes burnout and choking.

🎯 Everyday Example: An athlete with zero adrenaline plays sluggishly. An athlete with optimal pressure plays with hyper-focus. An athlete overwhelmed by panic makes clumsy errors.
💡 Key Lesson: Manage stress to stay in the 'Goldilocks zone' of peak focus.
More Info ↗
#23

Operant Conditioning

Rewards Repeat Behavior; Punishments Stop It

Habits & Motivation

B.F. Skinner demonstrated that behaviors followed by positive reinforcement (rewards) become habits, while behaviors followed by negative consequences die out.

🎯 Everyday Example: Social media apps use variable rewards (random likes, notifications) like slot machines to keep users endlessly pulling down to refresh their feed.
💡 Key Lesson: Design your habits by rewarding yourself immediately after completing difficult tasks.
More Info ↗
#24

The Zeigarnik Effect

Unfinished Tasks Keep Buzzing in Your Head

Habits & Motivation

Our brain remembers uncompleted or interrupted tasks much better than completed ones. An open loop creates mental tension that refuses to go away until resolved.

🎯 Everyday Example: TV shows always end on a cliffhanger so your brain cannot stop thinking about the next episode all week.
💡 Key Lesson: To beat procrastination, just start for 2 minutes. Once the task is open, your brain wants to finish it.
More Info ↗
#25

Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs

You Can't Write Poetry When You're Starving

Habits & Motivation

Human needs are arranged in a pyramid: physiological survival (food, water) comes first, followed by safety, love, self-esteem, and finally self-actualization.

🎯 Everyday Example: An employee terrified of losing their job (safety) cannot be motivated by high-minded corporate vision statements (self-actualization).
💡 Key Lesson: Take care of your basic sleep, food, and security before expecting peak creative genius.
More Info ↗
#26

Flow State (Csikszentmihalyi)

Complete Immersion Where Time Disappears

Habits & Motivation

A state of deep, effortless absorption where your skill level perfectly matches the difficulty of the challenge. You lose self-consciousness and time flies by.

🎯 Everyday Example: A programmer writing code for 4 straight hours without realizing the sun went down, completely locked in the rhythm.
💡 Key Lesson: Find flow by picking tasks slightly above your current comfort zone without distractions.
More Info ↗
#27

The Hawthorne Effect

People Work Better When Being Observed

Habits & Motivation

Individuals modify their behavior and increase productivity simply because they know they are being observed and given attention by others.

🎯 Everyday Example: Factory workers' productivity jumped when researchers observed them, whether the factory lights were turned brighter or dimmer.
💡 Key Lesson: Use accountability partners or public study sessions to instantly boost productivity.
More Info ↗
#28

Goodhart's Law

When a Measure Becomes a Target, It Ceases to Be a Good Measure

Habits & Motivation

Whenever you turn a simple performance metric into an explicit goal with rewards, people will find shortcuts to game the metric rather than doing genuine good work.

🎯 Everyday Example: A software team evaluated on 'number of lines of code written' begins writing bloated, copy-pasted code instead of clean, efficient solutions.
💡 Key Lesson: Align incentives with actual qualitative outcomes rather than easily gamed numbers.
More Info ↗
#29

Murphy's Law

Anything That Can Go Wrong, Will Go Wrong

Habits & Motivation

A philosophical and psychological adage about defensive thinking: given enough time and attempts, any vulnerability or potential point of failure will eventually fail.

🎯 Everyday Example: If you don't take a backup of your hard drive, the day your computer crashes will be the exact night before your final university thesis is due.
💡 Key Lesson: Build redundancy, have backups, and prepare for unexpected emergencies before they happen.
More Info ↗
#30

Self-Determination Theory

Autonomy, Competence, and Relatedness

Habits & Motivation

Deci and Ryan's theory proved that lasting human motivation doesn't come from carrots and sticks. It requires 3 psychological needs: feeling in control (Autonomy), feeling capable (Competence), and feeling connected (Relatedness).

🎯 Everyday Example: Open-source contributors write millions of lines of free software because they love the freedom to choose their tasks and build mastery with peers.
💡 Key Lesson: If you are unmotivated, check which leg of the stool is missing: autonomy, competence, or connection.

🧩 4. Memory, Perception & Learning
#31

Miller's Law

The Magic Number 7 (Plus or Minus 2)

Memory & Mind

George Miller discovered that human short-term working memory can only hold about 7 items (±2) at a single time before information starts dropping out.

🎯 Everyday Example: Phone numbers are divided into chunks (e.g. 555-019-2834) because memorizing 10 random continuous digits all at once overwhelms working memory.
💡 Key Lesson: Chunk complex information into groups of 3 to 5 items for easy recall.
More Info ↗
#32

Ebbinghaus Forgetting Curve

Memory Vanishes Fast Without Spaced Repetition

Memory & Mind

Without active review, humans forget over 50% of newly learned information within 1 hour, and up to 70% within 24 hours. Repeating it at increasing intervals flattens the curve.

🎯 Everyday Example: Cramming for an exam the night before lets you pass tomorrow, but two weeks later you won't remember 90% of the material.
💡 Key Lesson: Use spaced repetition flashcards (Day 1, Day 3, Day 7, Day 30) for lifelong memory.
More Info ↗
#33

Serial Position Effect

Remembering the First and the Last

Memory & Mind

When given a list, people remember the first few items (Primacy Effect) and the last few items (Recency Effect), while the middle items become a foggy blur.

🎯 Everyday Example: In a 10-person job interview schedule, candidates who interview first or last are remembered far better than those interviewed in the middle of the afternoon.
💡 Key Lesson: Put your most important points at the very beginning and very end of presentations.
More Info ↗
#34

The Peak-End Rule

We Judge Memories by the Climax and the Ending

Memory & Mind

People don't judge an experience by its average moment. We remember only two points: the most intense emotional moment (the peak) and how it ended.

🎯 Everyday Example: A 7-day vacation with 5 rainy days will be remembered fondly if day 6 had an amazing scuba dive (peak) and the last evening dinner was magical (end).
💡 Key Lesson: End meetings, dates, and experiences on a deliberate high note.
More Info ↗
#35

Mere-Exposure Effect

Familiarity Breeds Liking

Memory & Mind

People develop a preference for things merely because they are familiar with them. The more times we see someone or hear a song, the more we naturally like it.

🎯 Everyday Example: A pop song sounds weird the first time you hear it, but after the radio plays it 15 times, you find yourself tapping your foot and singing along.
💡 Key Lesson: Consistency in branding and showing up builds trust without needing a hard sell.
More Info ↗
#36

Cognitive Dissonance

The Mental Pain When Actions Clash with Beliefs

Memory & Mind

Leon Festinger showed that holding two contradictory beliefs (or acting against your values) causes uncomfortable mental distress. To stop the pain, we invent excuses.

🎯 Everyday Example: A person who knows smoking causes cancer tells themselves: 'My grandfather smoked and lived to 90, so it's probably fine for me!'
💡 Key Lesson: When you catch yourself rationalizing, pause and face the uncomfortable truth.
More Info ↗
#37

Fitts's Law

Bigger and Closer Targets Are Hit Faster

Memory & Mind

The time required to rapidly move to a target area is a function of the distance to the target and the width of the target.

🎯 Everyday Example: Smartphone apps place the primary action button (like 'Call' or 'Send') large and right at the bottom where your thumb naturally rests.
💡 Key Lesson: Make desired actions huge and close at hand; make destructive actions small and far away.
More Info ↗
#38

Weber-Fechner Law

Perception is Relative, Not Absolute

Memory & Mind

The noticeable change in a stimulus is a constant ratio of the original stimulus. Our senses detect percentage differences, not raw amounts.

🎯 Everyday Example: If you hold a 100g weight, adding 10g is noticeable. But if you hold a 20kg barbell, adding 10g is completely imperceptible.
💡 Key Lesson: People will drive 15 minutes to save $10 on a $25 toaster, but won't drive 15 minutes to save $10 on a $1,000 laptop.
More Info ↗
#39

Schema Theory

The Mental Templates That Guide Our World

Memory & Mind

Schemas are mental building blocks of knowledge that categorize information. When you enter a classroom or hospital, you instantly know how to act without thinking.

🎯 Everyday Example: When someone mentions a 'birthday party', your mind instantly pictures cake, candles, gifts, and singing, even without anyone describing them.
💡 Key Lesson: New ideas are learned best when anchored to a schema people already understand.
More Info ↗
#40

False Memory Effect

Memories Can Be Altered and Invented

Memory & Mind

Elizabeth Loftus showed that human memory is not a video recording. It is a creative reconstruction that can easily be contaminated by suggestive questions and imagination.

🎯 Everyday Example: Witnesses asked 'How fast were cars going when they smashed into each other?' remembered broken glass that was never actually present at the accident scene.
💡 Key Lesson: Never rely solely on memory for vital details; keep written logs and receipts.

🌱 5. Emotion, Stress & Resilience
#41

Learned Helplessness

Giving Up When You Believe You Have No Control

Emotion & Stress

Martin Seligman discovered that when humans or animals endure repeated painful events with no way to escape, they eventually stop trying to escape even when the door is opened.

🎯 Everyday Example: A student who fails multiple math exams concludes 'I am born bad at math' and stops studying altogether, even when given an easy tutor.
💡 Key Lesson: Break the helplessness cycle by scoring small, guaranteed micro-victories to regain your agency.
More Info ↗
#42

Negativity Bias

Bad News Weighs 3x More Than Good News

Emotion & Stress

For ancestral survival, avoiding a predator was more important than finding sweet fruit. So our brains evolved to react much more strongly to negative events than positive ones.

🎯 Everyday Example: You receive 10 glowing compliments and 1 mild criticism at work. You spend the whole weekend ruminating on the single criticism.
💡 Key Lesson: Deliberately celebrate wins and give yourself 5 positive reminders to balance 1 critique.
More Info ↗
#43

The Hedonic Treadmill

Happiness Always Returns to Baseline

Emotion & Stress

Humans quickly adapt to major positive or negative life events. After the initial thrill of a promotion or a new car, your happiness quickly returns to your default set point.

🎯 Everyday Example: Lottery winners experience a surge of ecstasy for a few months, but studies show their daily happiness returns to normal within a year.
💡 Key Lesson: Lasting fulfillment comes from purpose, growth, and relationships, not endless material upgrades.
More Info ↗
#44

Impostor Syndrome

Feeling Like a Fake Despite Proven Success

Emotion & Stress

High-achieving individuals often doubt their accomplishments and suffer a persistent internalized fear of being exposed as an unqualified 'fraud'.

🎯 Everyday Example: A brilliant software engineer who built complex features doubts their skills and believes they only got the job through luck or timing.
💡 Key Lesson: Almost everyone at the top feels this way. Self-doubt is often proof that you care about high standards.
More Info ↗
#45

Cognitive Reappraisal

Changing the Meaning Changes the Emotion

Emotion & Stress

An emotion regulation strategy where you consciously reinterpret the meaning of a stressful situation to alter its emotional impact.

🎯 Everyday Example: Instead of thinking 'The flight delay ruined my trip and I want to scream!', thinking: 'I finally have 3 quiet hours to read my favorite book.'
💡 Key Lesson: You cannot always control what happens, but you have 100% control over how you frame it.
